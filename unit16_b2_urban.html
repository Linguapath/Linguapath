الكتابة — IELTS Writing Task 2

IELTS · TASK 2

📋 Conditional Inversions في IELTS Writing

  • Had cities invested in public transport earlier, congestion would not have reached today's levels.
  • Were governments to prioritise green spaces, urban wellbeing would improve significantly.
  • Should urban planners adopt a people-first approach, the results could be transformative.
  • Had zoning laws been less restrictive, housing affordability would not be the crisis it is today.
Cars should be banned from city centres. To what extent do you agree?
يجب حظر السيارات من مراكز المدن. إلى أي مدى توافق؟
I broadly agree that reducing private car access to city centres is both necessary and beneficial. Had cities designed their cores around pedestrians rather than vehicles, the relationship between urban space and human activity would have developed very differently. Were car access to be restricted progressively — through congestion charges, expanded pedestrian zones, and genuine investment in public transport — the quality of urban life for residents would improve considerably. That said, an outright ban raises legitimate concerns: those who depend on cars for mobility, whether due to disability, caring responsibilities, or inadequate public transport, would be disproportionately disadvantaged. Should a city introduce restrictions without simultaneously improving alternatives, it risks penalising the most vulnerable rather than the most affluent. The goal should be not the punishment of drivers but the creation of conditions in which not driving becomes the more attractive option.
Governments should prioritise building new cities rather than expanding existing ones. Do you agree?
يجب أن تُولي الحكومات الأولوية لبناء مدن جديدة بدلاً من توسيع القائمة. هل توافق؟
The appeal of building entirely new cities is understandable: had planners been given the opportunity to design from scratch rather than retrofitting infrastructure around historical constraints, the outcomes might have been more rational and sustainable. However, the historical record of purpose-built cities is decidedly mixed. Were new cities to automatically succeed, Brazil's Brasília and Nigeria's Abuja would not have developed the informal settlements and traffic dysfunction that have come to characterise them. The fundamental challenge is that cities are not simply buildings and roads — they are accumulated social and economic relationships that take generations to develop. Should governments instead invest equivalent resources in improving public transport, greening existing urban areas, and making housing affordable in existing cities, the benefits would reach far more people far sooner. I therefore largely disagree: retrofitting existing cities, though harder, typically serves populations better than starting from zero.
Urban sprawl is one of the most serious problems facing modern cities. To what extent do you agree?
الزحف العمراني من أشد المشاكل التي تواجه المدن الحديثة. إلى أي مدى توافق؟
Urban sprawl — the uncontrolled outward expansion of cities into surrounding land — is a genuine and underappreciated problem. Had zoning laws in many cities been designed to encourage density rather than disperse it, the car dependency, long commute times, and loss of agricultural land that define sprawling urban areas would have been significantly limited. Were cities to develop more compactly around transit corridors, the environmental footprint of urban living could be dramatically reduced. The costs of sprawl are not merely environmental: the provision of infrastructure — roads, utilities, schools — across low-density suburban areas is enormously expensive for governments relative to the population served. Should sprawl continue unchecked, these fiscal pressures will compound. That said, not all low-density development is problematic: the desire for space, green surroundings, and affordable housing is entirely legitimate. The challenge is to accommodate growth in ways that do not compromise either sustainability or affordability.
The gentrification of urban areas causes more harm than good. To what extent do you agree?
التجديد العمراني يضرّ أكثر مما ينفع. إلى أي مدى توافق؟
Gentrification — the process by which neglected urban areas attract wealthier residents, raising property values and displacing established communities — presents genuine benefits alongside well-documented harms. Had investment in urban renewal been structured to protect existing residents, the improvements in safety, amenity, and public services that gentrification often brings could have been more equitably shared. Were displacement protections — rent controls, community land trusts, affordable housing requirements — embedded in planning policy from the outset, the benefits of renewal need not come at the cost of those who built the community in the first place. The harm lies not in improvement itself but in who bears its costs and who captures its gains. Should urban renewal policies be designed with equity as a central objective rather than an afterthought, the transformation of neglected areas need not require the dispersal of their existing populations. I therefore believe gentrification in its current form causes more harm than good — but that this is a policy failure, not an inevitable feature of urban change.
Green spaces in cities are a luxury that cannot be justified given the demand for housing. Do you agree?
المساحات الخضراء في المدن رفاهية لا يمكن تبريرها مع الطلب على السكن. هل توافق؟
The framing of green spaces as luxuries reflects a misunderstanding of what cities are for. Had urban planning been guided purely by the logic of housing supply, the cities produced would have been, as historical examples demonstrate, deeply hostile to human flourishing. Were green spaces to be systematically removed to make way for housing, the mental and physical health costs — thoroughly documented in urban health research — would themselves impose significant economic burdens on public services. The argument rests on a false trade-off: housing density and green space are not inherently incompatible. Should cities build upward rather than outward, and design public green infrastructure intelligently, both can be provided without sacrifice. I firmly disagree with the premise: green spaces are not luxuries but necessities — as important to the liveability of a city as roads or sewers, and as deserving of investment.
