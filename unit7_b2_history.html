الكتابة — IELTS Writing Task 2

مقالات أكاديمية عن التاريخ والحضارة
IELTS · TASK 2

📋 Narrative Distance في IELTS Writing

  • Past Perfect لترتيب الأحداث: "By the time Rome fell, it had already declined for centuries."
  • used to / would للعادات الماضية: "Ancient societies used to record events in stone; scribes would carve for years."
  • للتقييم التاريخي: "What history teaches us is that no civilisation is permanent."
  • اربط الماضي بالحاضر: "Civilisations that ignored these warnings collapsed; the lesson remains."
Some people believe that studying history is a waste of time. Others argue it is essential. Discuss both views and give your opinion.
هل دراسة التاريخ مضيعة للوقت أم ضرورة؟ (Discuss both views)
Those who dismiss history argue that the past has little to teach a rapidly changing world; what mattered to ancient societies, they claim, had no bearing on the challenges we face today. This view is not without merit. By the time we apply historical lessons, circumstances have often changed beyond recognition. Nevertheless, I firmly believe that history is essential. Civilisations that had ignored the warning signs of their own decline — environmental collapse, inequality, overreach — repeatedly fell, and the patterns recur. What history teaches us is not a set of predictions but a way of thinking: an awareness that no present is permanent and no power eternal. To abandon the study of the past is to forfeit the only laboratory we have for understanding how societies rise, endure, and fall.
Historical buildings and monuments should be preserved at any cost. To what extent do you agree?
يجب الحفاظ على المباني والآثار التاريخية مهما كلّف. إلى أي مدى توافق؟ (Opinion)
Historical monuments are the physical memory of civilisation; once they are lost, no reconstruction can fully restore what had taken centuries to create. For this reason, I largely agree that preservation matters profoundly. Societies that destroyed their heritage — whether through war, neglect, or ideology — severed a connection to the people who had come before them. That said, the phrase 'at any cost' demands scrutiny. Resources are finite, and a community struggling with poverty cannot reasonably be asked to prioritise ancient stones over living people. The wiser principle is that heritage should be preserved wherever possible, integrated into modern life rather than frozen in time. What we inherit from the past, we hold in trust for those who will come after us.
Why do civilisations collapse, and what can modern societies learn from this?
لماذا تنهار الحضارات وما الذي تتعلمه المجتمعات الحديثة؟ (Problem/Solution)
History reveals that civilisations rarely collapse for a single reason. By the time a society fails, it has usually accumulated multiple stresses: environmental degradation that had exhausted its resources, inequality that had hollowed out its cohesion, and leadership that had grown detached from reality. The Maya, the Romans, and the inhabitants of Easter Island all faced versions of these pressures. What modern societies can learn is sobering. We, too, are placing unsustainable demands on our environment; we, too, are experiencing rising inequality. The lesson of history is not that collapse is inevitable, but that it becomes likely when societies ignore the warnings their own predecessors left behind. Those who study the past are not condemned to repeat it — but those who ignore it almost always are.
Some argue that history is written by the victors and cannot be trusted. Do you agree?
هل التاريخ يكتبه المنتصرون ولا يمكن الوثوق به؟ (Opinion)
There is undeniable truth in the claim that history has often been written by those who held power. For most of recorded time, the people who controlled the records were the same people who had won the wars; consequently, the voices of the defeated were frequently silenced or distorted. That said, I do not accept that this renders history untrustworthy. Modern historians have developed methods — archaeology, comparative sources, the analysis of material culture — that allow them to recover what official records once concealed. What used to be a single triumphant narrative is increasingly being challenged by evidence the victors never imagined would survive. History, in other words, is not a fixed text but an ongoing argument, one that grows more honest as new evidence emerges.
Globalisation is erasing local cultures and traditions. To what extent do you agree?
العولمة تمحو الثقافات والتقاليد المحلية. إلى أي مدى توافق؟ (Opinion)
There is real cause for concern that globalisation is homogenising the world's cultures. Practices that communities used to pass down through generations — languages, crafts, rituals — are vanishing at an alarming rate; by some estimates, a language dies every two weeks. Cultures that had developed in isolation for millennia are being absorbed into a single global marketplace. That said, the picture is not entirely bleak. Globalisation has also allowed endangered traditions to find new audiences and advocates worldwide. What had once been confined to a single village can now be shared globally. My view is that globalisation threatens cultural diversity but does not doom it. The civilisations that thrive will be those that learn to engage with the world without dissolving into it.
