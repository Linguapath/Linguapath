الكتابة — IELTS Writing Task 2

مقالات عن الأخلاق في العالم الحديث
IELTS · TASK 2

📋 Cleft Sentences في IELTS Writing

  • للتأكيد: "It is accountability, not punishment, that makes ethical systems work."
  • للتباين: "It is not wealth but character that defines a person's worth."
  • للموضوع: "What concerns ethicists most is the absence of enforceable global standards."
  • للخلاصة: "What the evidence ultimately shows is that wellbeing requires connection."
  • للتضييق: "All that is needed for ethical failure is the absence of accountability."
Is it ever acceptable to lie? Discuss different views and give your opinion.
هل من المقبول أحياناً الكذب؟ ناقش وجهات نظر مختلفة وأعطِ رأيك.
It is the absolutist position — that lying is always wrong — that most people reject when they consider particular cases. What the trolley problem and similar thought experiments reveal is that our moral intuitions are not rule-based but contextual: most people believe lying to protect an innocent person from a murderer is not only permissible but required. Deontologists, following Kant, argue that it is the act of deception itself that matters, not its consequences. What consequentialists counter is that it is outcomes — their effects on real people — that give acts their moral character. I believe that lying is generally wrong, not because rules are sacred, but because trust is the foundation on which all productive human relationships rest. What distinguishes permissible from impermissible deception is not merely intention but whether the lie protects or exploits the person deceived.
Wealthy people have a moral obligation to give to charity. To what extent do you agree?
الأثرياء لديهم التزام أخلاقي بالتبرّع. إلى أي مدى توافق؟
It is the philosopher Peter Singer who makes perhaps the strongest case for a moral obligation to give: if we can prevent serious harm without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, it is our duty to do so. What makes this argument uncomfortable is precisely its generality — it applies not just to the very rich but to anyone with disposable income in a world of preventable suffering. That said, what concerns many critics is not the obligation itself but how charity is structured. What Dan Pallotta argues, compellingly, is that it is not how much money charities raise but how efficiently they deploy it that determines their impact — and that current models systematically limit both. I believe wealthy individuals do bear a moral obligation to give, but what matters most is the quality of their giving — whether it is structured to produce lasting change rather than temporary relief.
The ends justify the means. To what extent do you agree with this ethical view?
الغاية تبرّر الوسيلة. إلى أي مدى توافق مع هذا الموقف الأخلاقي؟
It is the consequentialist tradition that underlies the claim that ends justify means: if an act produces good outcomes, that is what makes it morally right. What makes this position appealing is its intuitive commonsense — most of us do weigh consequences in our moral reasoning. However, what history demonstrates is that the principle, taken seriously, licenses atrocities: it was precisely the logic that ends justify means that justified torture, forced displacement, and mass sacrifice in the name of progress. What Kant's deontological ethics counters is that it is not consequences but the nature of the act that determines its morality — treating persons as ends in themselves, never merely as means. I believe a defensible ethics must acknowledge consequences but cannot be wholly determined by them. What it requires, ultimately, is the recognition that some acts are simply impermissible regardless of their projected outcomes.
Is happiness the most important thing in life? Discuss.
هل السعادة أهم شيء في الحياة؟ ناقش.
It is a common philosophical error to treat happiness as an end rather than a byproduct. What the longest scientific study of human wellbeing — Harvard's eighty-year Grant Study — reveals is that it is the quality of our relationships, not wealth or achievement, that predicts flourishing across a lifetime. What this suggests is that happiness is not found by pursuing it directly but by investing in what produces it: meaningful work, deep relationships, and the sense that one's life has purpose. What concerns me about the claim that happiness is the most important thing is its tendency to license a passive form of self-indulgence. It is not pleasure but meaning — the sense that what we do and suffer matters — that appears to be the deepest human need. What Aristotle called eudaimonia — flourishing — is perhaps closer to what most people mean by the good life than what most people mean by happiness.
Animals should have the same rights as humans. To what extent do you agree?
يجب أن تتمتّع الحيوانات بنفس حقوق البشر. إلى أي مدى توافق؟
It is the capacity for suffering, not the capacity for reason, that most animal ethicists argue grounds moral consideration. What Peter Singer argues is that it is speciesism — the assumption that human interests automatically outweigh animal ones — that licenses our current treatment of animals rather than any morally coherent principle. What I find persuasive in this view is the basic point: suffering is suffering regardless of the species experiencing it, and the ability to articulate one's interests in language does not determine their moral weight. However, what the claim that animals should have the same rights as humans risks obscuring is the real and important differences between humans and other animals in moral agency and social complexity. It is not identical rights but adequate protection from suffering and exploitation that animals most urgently need — a goal that is achievable without requiring the implausible claim that all species stand in an equal moral position.
