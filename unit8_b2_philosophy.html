الكتابة — IELTS Writing Task 2

مقالات أكاديمية عن فلسفة العقل
IELTS · TASK 2

📋 Comment Clauses في IELTS Writing

  • للتحفّظ: "Free will, I would argue, is more complex than it appears."
  • للإحالة: "Consciousness, as Descartes famously argued, raises the mind-body problem."
  • للإشارة للمصدر: "As far as neuroscience can tell, all mental states have physical correlates."
  • للتأكيد: "It seems to me that the question is not whether the mind exists, but what it is."
Do humans have free will, or are our actions determined by biology and environment?
هل يمتلك البشر إرادة حرة أم أن أفعالنا محدَّدة مسبقاً؟ (Discuss / Opinion)
The question of free will is, I would argue, the most practically urgent in philosophy — because it underlies our entire framework of moral responsibility. Those who hold a strictly determinist view contend that every decision we make is the inevitable product of prior causes: genetics, upbringing, and neural processes beyond conscious access. It must be admitted that the evidence from neuroscience is troubling. As far as we can tell, the brain initiates action before the conscious mind is aware of deciding. That said, it seems to me that this does not settle the matter. Consciousness, as many philosophers believe, may play a role in evaluating and redirecting impulses rather than simply executing them. In my view, the question is not whether we are free in some absolute sense, but whether the kind of self-reflection we are capable of is sufficient to ground moral responsibility. I believe it is.
Is consciousness more than just brain activity? Discuss.
هل الوعي أكثر من مجرد نشاط دماغي؟ ناقش. (Discussion)
Consciousness is, it seems to me, the hardest problem in science — not because we lack data, but because no amount of data about neurons can, as far as we currently know, fully explain the subjective experience of being. This is what the philosopher David Chalmers calls the 'hard problem': why does physical brain activity feel like anything at all? Those who adopt a purely materialist view argue that consciousness is simply what the brain does; there is nothing more to explain. I would argue, however, that this view sidesteps rather than solves the question. The experience of seeing red, of feeling pain, of having a thought — these, it must be admitted, are not obviously captured by a neural description alone. Whether this means consciousness transcends the brain, or simply that our current concepts are inadequate, remains, in my view, genuinely open.
Can animals think? To what extent do you agree that thought requires language?
هل تستطيع الحيوانات التفكير؟ هل يستلزم التفكير اللغة؟ (Opinion)
The question of whether animals think depends, I would argue, entirely on what we mean by thinking. If thought requires language in the full human sense, then, as far as we know, only humans qualify. But this definition seems to me needlessly restrictive. Cognitive science has shown that many species demonstrate problem-solving, tool use, and what appears to be anticipatory planning — capacities that, it could be argued, constitute a form of thought without language. The crow that bends a wire to retrieve food is, it seems to me, doing something that deserves the name. That said, it must be admitted that the richness of abstract thought — imagining hypothetical futures, reflecting on one's own existence — does appear to require something very close to language. My view is that thought exists on a spectrum, and that the honest answer is not 'yes' or 'no' but 'to what degree?'
Are emotions a strength or a weakness in human decision-making?
هل المشاعر قوة أم ضعف في صنع القرار البشري؟ (Discuss / Opinion)
The traditional view, as philosophers from Plato to Kant argued, is that reason should govern emotion. Emotions, on this account, distort judgement; the rational agent is one who, it seems to me, has learned to set feeling aside. But this view, I would argue, is mistaken — or at least dangerously incomplete. As the neuroscientist Antonio Damasio has shown, patients who suffer damage to the brain's emotional centres become incapable of making decisions at all, even when their reasoning faculties remain intact. It must be admitted, then, that emotion and cognition are not opposed but deeply entangled. Emotions, it seems, provide the value signals that make decisions possible. That said, not all emotions improve decision-making equally; fear and anger, in particular, can bias judgement in ways that damage both the individual and others. In my view, the goal is not to suppress emotion but to cultivate the right ones.
Is it ethical to develop artificial intelligence that simulates human consciousness?
هل من الأخلاقي تطوير ذكاء اصطناعي يُحاكي الوعي البشري؟ (Ethics/Opinion)
The ethical question raised by conscious AI is, I would argue, among the most serious we face — and it hinges on a prior philosophical question: what is consciousness, and can it be manufactured? As far as we currently know, no AI system is genuinely conscious; what we call 'simulated consciousness' is, it seems to me, the appearance of inner life rather than the thing itself. Nevertheless, it must be admitted that we do not fully understand what consciousness is in biological systems either. If we were to create an entity that genuinely experienced suffering or joy, the moral implications would be profound. The question, as I see it, is not whether to develop AI but how to do so responsibly — with the humility to acknowledge what we do not know, and the caution to avoid creating minds we are not prepared to take moral responsibility for.
