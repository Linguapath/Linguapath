الكتابة — IELTS Writing Task 2

مقالات عن الغذاء والاستدامة
IELTS · TASK 2

📋 Register في IELTS Writing

  • دائماً رسمي في IELTS Task 2: لا contractions، لا "you know"، لا "basically"
  • بدل "start" → "commence / initiate / begin to"
  • بدل "use" → "utilise / employ / apply"
  • بدل "show" → "demonstrate / reveal / indicate"
  • بدل "need" → "require / necessitate / demand"
  • Nominalisation: "because we eat meat" → "as a consequence of meat consumption"
People should reduce their meat consumption to protect the environment. To what extent do you agree?
يجب على الناس تقليص استهلاك اللحوم لحماية البيئة. إلى أي مدى توافق؟
The environmental case for reducing meat consumption is among the most robustly evidenced in the sustainability literature. Animal agriculture accounts for a substantial share of global greenhouse gas emissions, demands disproportionate quantities of land and water relative to the nutritional output produced, and is a primary driver of deforestation in many regions. The dietary shift required to address these impacts need not be absolute: a significant reduction in the consumption of the most resource-intensive meats — principally beef and lamb — would deliver substantial environmental benefits even without full vegetarianism. That said, the framing of this as an individual responsibility, while accurate, risks obscuring the structural dimension: industrial food systems that make plant-based diets more expensive and less convenient than meat-heavy alternatives create conditions in which individual choice is severely constrained. I broadly agree that reducing meat consumption is necessary — but I would emphasise that systemic change in how food is produced, priced, and promoted is at least as important as individual dietary modification.
Governments should tax unhealthy food to encourage healthier eating habits. To what extent do you agree?
يجب على الحكومات فرض ضريبة على الطعام غير الصحي لتشجيع العادات الغذائية الصحية. إلى أي مدى توافق؟
The taxation of unhealthy food represents one of the more contested instruments of public health policy. The evidence base for its effectiveness is real but qualified: sugar taxes, for instance, have demonstrably reduced consumption of the targeted products in several countries, though substitution towards other unhealthy products limits the aggregate health impact. The objection most frequently raised is that such taxes are regressive — falling disproportionately on lower-income households for whom cheap, processed food constitutes a larger share of total expenditure. This concern is legitimate and should inform the design of any tax rather than preclude it: a tax whose revenues are directed towards subsidising healthy food in deprived communities addresses the distributive concern while retaining the incentive effect. I broadly agree that taxation of unhealthy food is a legitimate policy instrument — provided it is designed with equity as a central consideration rather than an afterthought.
Food waste is a greater problem than food scarcity. To what extent do you agree?
هدر الطعام مشكلة أكبر من شحّه. إلى أي مدى توافق؟
The comparison between food waste and food scarcity presents a false hierarchy that obscures a more important relationship: they are connected symptoms of the same dysfunctional food system. Approximately one third of all food produced globally is wasted, while hundreds of millions of people experience food insecurity — not because insufficient food exists, but because the distribution mechanisms and economic incentives of the global food system fail to direct it to those who need it. Food waste occurs disproportionately in wealthy countries, at the retail and consumer stages; food scarcity is concentrated in lower-income regions, driven by structural poverty, inadequate infrastructure, and climate vulnerability. To rank these problems is less productive than to recognise that addressing waste in wealthy countries does not automatically resolve scarcity in poorer ones — and that both require systemic intervention at the level of production, distribution, and economic policy rather than individual behavioural change alone.
Organic farming is the solution to the problems caused by industrial agriculture. To what extent do you agree?
الزراعة العضوية هي الحل للمشاكل التي سبّبتها الزراعة الصناعية. إلى أي مدى توافق؟
Organic farming addresses some of the most serious environmental problems associated with industrial agriculture — soil degradation, pesticide contamination, biodiversity loss — and does so through principles of production that deserve wider adoption. However, to present it as the solution overstates what the evidence supports. Organic yields are typically 20-25% lower than conventional ones, which implies that universal adoption would require significantly more land to produce the same quantity of food — potentially worsening deforestation pressures. Furthermore, the premium pricing of organic food limits its accessibility to higher-income consumers, making it an inadequate vehicle for broader dietary transition. The more defensible argument is that specific practices associated with organic farming — crop rotation, reduced pesticide use, protection of soil microbiomes — should be incorporated into reformed conventional agriculture, rather than treating organic as a discrete alternative sector. The goal should be a food system that is both productive and regenerative, which may require elements of both approaches rather than wholesale adoption of either.
It is the responsibility of individuals, not governments, to ensure a healthy diet. To what extent do you agree?
المسؤولية تقع على الأفراد لا الحكومات في ضمان اتّباع نظام غذائي صحي. إلى أي مدى توافق؟
The premise that individuals bear primary responsibility for their dietary choices rests on an assumption of free choice that the food environment in most countries does not support. Ultra-processed foods are heavily marketed to children, are priced below healthier alternatives, and are far more conveniently available in many communities than fresh produce. In this context, to assign primary responsibility to individuals is to mistake constrained choice for free choice. This does not mean that individuals bear no responsibility — but it does mean that the structural conditions within which choices are made matter enormously. Governments have both the capacity and the obligation to shape the food environment: through regulation of marketing, product reformulation requirements, taxation of harmful products, and subsidisation of healthy ones. I therefore largely disagree with the premise: while individual agency is real and important, it operates within constraints that only collective action — through government — can meaningfully address. Framing dietary health as primarily individual is not merely factually questionable; it is politically convenient for those who benefit from the current food environment remaining unchanged.
